Partner Service Portal

A role-based portal for external service partners to accept work, manage service obligations and stay compatible with the internal operations model without inheriting its complexity.

My Role
Product / UX Designer
Users
Service partners
Timeline
Assumed 10–14 weeks
Surface
Responsive web

Because of company policy and NDA constraints, some end-to-end screens and analytics cannot be shown. The case study focuses on the problem, research, design decisions, and selected interface glimpses. Diagrams below are original illustrations of the reconstructed model, not reproductions of the source screens.

01

Context: extend the service operation beyond the company boundary

The Partner portal archive contains early paper sketches, partner/brand references, service-category imagery and Onsitego sign-in/context assets. I read this as a workspace for external service partners who need controlled access to jobs, devices and commercial/service information without exposing the entire internal operations system.

02

The product tension: give partners enough control without giving them internal-system complexity

PARTNER NEED

Know what to do next

See assigned jobs, required evidence, due dates and service expectations.

BUSINESS NEED

Protect consistency

Ensure external teams follow the same service states and proof standards as internal teams.

PLATFORM NEED

Limit complexity

Expose only the data and actions a partner is authorized to see.

03

Decision 1 — make the dashboard a work queue, not a reporting homepage

For external partners, "dashboard" should mean today's obligations: jobs awaiting acceptance, appointments due, evidence rejected, invoices/payouts needing action and jobs blocked by customer or part availability. Reporting remains secondary.

04

Decision 2 — progressive disclosure by partner role

A service-center owner, coordinator and field technician do not need the same portal. Role-based navigation reduces training burden and protects sensitive commercial information. The owner sees performance and payouts; the coordinator sees job queues and staffing; the technician sees only assigned work and evidence requirements.

Reconstructed access model. The same underlying job record is projected differently per role, so a technician never sees payout terms and an owner is never forced through a technician's evidence checklist.
05

Decision 3 — use a shared service vocabulary across internal and external tools

The highest-risk failure is terminology drift: an internal system says "assigned," a partner calls it "accepted," and finance sees "open." I would normalize the lifecycle into a shared state model and reuse the same status language, timestamps and exception reasons across the partner portal and internal operations system.

Design system implication

Shared components, different permissions

The portal should inherit core tables, status chips, filters and service-detail patterns from the internal system while applying a thinner role-appropriate shell. That creates consistency without cloning internal complexity.

06

Trust and accountability, not just access

EVIDENCE STANDARD

Same proof bar as internal teams

Partners submit the same photo, note and diagnosis evidence internal engineers do — the portal should reject incomplete submissions the same way, not silently accept less.

SLA VISIBILITY

The clock is always on screen

Acceptance and service SLAs are shown as a countdown, not a policy document, so a coordinator can prioritize by risk of breach.

DISPUTE PATH

A rejected invoice needs a reason

When a payout is held, the portal states which evidence or field triggered the hold, so a partner can resolve it without calling support.

ONBOARDING

Read-only until verified

New partner accounts see the portal in a limited, read-only mode until credentials and service-area agreements are confirmed.

Process

From internal complexity to a partner-ready shell

01

Map the boundary

Identify exactly which internal data and actions a partner is authorized to touch.

02

Define roles

Separate owner, coordinator and technician needs instead of one generic "partner user."

03

Reuse the design system

Pull status chips, tables and filters from the internal ELAN system rather than inventing new ones.

04

Normalize vocabulary

Align state names across partner and internal tools to prevent terminology drift.

05

Design accountability

Build evidence standards, SLA countdowns and dispute paths into the core flow, not as an afterthought.

06

Sequence onboarding

Define what a partner sees before verification is complete.

Outcome

A portal that feels purpose-built for partners while staying structurally identical, underneath, to the system operations already trusts.

08

Reflection

A partner portal succeeds when it feels simpler than the internal product while still producing data the internal product can trust. The design goal is not feature parity; it is operational compatibility with much lower cognitive load.

The recurring tension worth naming is that "simpler" and "compatible" pull in opposite directions — every field removed to reduce partner complexity is a field the internal system might still expect. Resolving that tension role by role, rather than portal-wide, is what keeps the shell thin without breaking the data internal teams depend on.
