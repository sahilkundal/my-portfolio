Field Management System

Turning a dense service-operations console into an exception-first command center for dispatching, reassignment and SLA recovery.

01

Context: hundreds of jobs, one operations team, no room for ambiguity

The Field Management System + Livtrack 2024 archive contains dense job tables, queue views, reassignment states, modal controls, sorting/filtering patterns and whiteboard system maps. The product reads as an operations command center for coordinating field-service work at scale — the desktop counterpart to the mobile experience covered in the Livtrack Pro case study.

The core UX challenge was not displaying more service data. It was helping an operator answer three questions immediately: What is stuck? Why is it stuck? What can I do about it?

02

Users and jobs-to-be-done

OPERATIONS

Dispatch coordinators

Find unassigned or delayed jobs, match the right engineer, and keep appointments moving.

MANAGEMENT

Regional / service managers

Understand queue health, bottlenecks and SLA risk across teams.

FIELD

Service engineers

Receive clear assignments and avoid unnecessary reassignment or escalation loops.

Reading the artifact set as a job-to-be-done rather than a screen inventory reframes the console: a dispatcher does not want "a table of jobs," they want the next job that needs a human decision, surfaced without a manual scan.

03

How a job actually moves through the system

Before proposing a layout, it helps to make the underlying state model explicit. Most of a job's life is uneventful — assigned, en route, in progress, closed — but the interface earns its keep in the branches: what happens when a job goes off-track.

Reconstructed state model for a service job, based on the reassignment and status artifacts in the archive. The branch into "At risk / blocked" is where the console needs to do the most work.
04

Decision 1 — organize the product around operational exceptions

A standard CRM-style table makes every row look equally important. But an operator's real job is exception handling. I would use queue status, SLA state, assignment health and blocking reason as the primary hierarchy, with customer and device details supporting that decision rather than competing with it.

Information hierarchy

Signal first, detail second

Color/status treatments should be reserved for operational meaning — overdue, blocked, unassigned, waiting — not decorative categorization. Secondary details can remain available through row expansion or a side panel.

05

Decision 2 — make reassignment a guided workflow

The Figma artifact includes modal states for changing service assignment. This is a high-cost action: the wrong reassignment can create missed appointments, duplicate travel or SLA breaches. I would structure the modal around the operator's decision rather than a raw dropdown list.

02Show eligible engineers
03Expose distance / capacity / skill
04Confirm customer impact
06

Decision 3 — connect queues to the mental model operators already use

The artifact includes board-like queue groupings and dense filtered states. That suggests operators were already thinking in operational buckets. The design opportunity is to turn those buckets into saved, action-oriented work queues: "needs assignment," "appointment at risk," "awaiting part," "customer reschedule," and similar slices that map directly to daily work.

08

Reflection

Dense enterprise interfaces are not automatically bad. In operational software, density is often a feature — as long as the hierarchy reflects the decisions people make. The redesign principle here is make the next intervention obvious without hiding the context an expert operator needs.

The harder lesson is that exception handling is a spectrum, not a binary. A job that is "at risk" today and "blocked" tomorrow needs a console that reflects gradual degradation, not just a green/red status chip — which is why the state model in section 03 treats risk as a branch with its own recovery path, rather than a dead end.
